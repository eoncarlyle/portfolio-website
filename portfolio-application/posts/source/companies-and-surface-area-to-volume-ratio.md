# Companies and Surface-Area-to-Volume-Ratio


When you place a drop of food colouring into a glass of water and let it sit
long enough, the colouring will disperse evenly in the glass. Passive diffusion
is responsible for this dispersion, and this is this is important to cells in
multi-cellular organisms. Rather than actively grabbing food from their
surroundings they rely on passive diffusion for nutrients to reach the cell.

If you're a muscle cell in a run-of-the-mill vertebrate this has a lot of
advantages because you don't need to spend energy to get energy. The bloodstream
has a higher concentration of nutrients than the cell itself, so passive
diffusion moves them from outside the cell to inside the cell. The downside of
passive diffusion is that there are limits on how large you can be. The rate of
food and other vital chemicals diffusing into the cell increases with the
surface area, but said molecules take more time to diffuse through larger
volumes. If you roughly approximate a cell as a sphere, surface area is
proportional to the square of a radius while volume is proportional to the
_cube_ of the radius. A smaller cell with a higher surface-area-to-volume-ratio
(SA:V) can get nutrients to where they need to go with time to spare while
larger cells at a lower SA:V will struggle to feed themselves.

SA:V provides some useful analogies to firms. You can think of the nucleus as
upper management and the outer cell membrane as where interactions with
customers and the greater outside world take place. For a construction firm
outer membrane would be a job site, for a grocery store it could be the checkout
aisle, and for a warehouse logistics company it would be the loading dock. But
regardless of industry, a ten person company is a lot like a small cell with a
high SA:V because every employee is close to the 'surface area' of the outside
world and the work that is done to deliver on the firm's value proposition to
customers. The owner of a neighbourhood auto repair shop can often literally see
the shop floor from their office, and at a small manufacturer it doesn't take
that much time for word of a serious problem to reach company leadership whether
it came from employees on the line or from a customer.

The larger a company grows, the more of its parts are not directly tied to the
core work of the company: Google used to employ [ex-Pixar
screenwriters](https://www.wired.com/story/the-human-in-google-assistant/) to
make Google Assistant more engaging, and Tinder used to have an [in-house
sociologist](https://www.drjesscarbino.com/#about). Both of these jobs bring
value to the company, but the explanation as to _how_ they bring value is more
complicated than it would be be for any job at a 10 person company. Even for
less unusual roles, the distance that most employees have from where the value
is being created and how long it takes feedback from the outside world to reach
them can drive diseconomies of scale. If an indie developer unknowingly breaks a
user's workflow they are likely to find out much faster than the developers
responsible for the depths of Microsoft Word: just validating a bug and routing
it to the right team isn't trivial at a large organisation and unless something
is seriously broken it can take a surprising amount of time.
