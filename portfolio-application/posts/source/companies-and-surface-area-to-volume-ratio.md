# Companies and Surface-Area-to-Volume-Ratio

Most cells in a multi-cellular organism do not actively grab nutrients from
their surroundings, but rather rely on passive diffusion for nutrients to reach
the cell. This is the same physical phenomena responsible for food colouring
dispersing itself evenly when placed into a glass of water and left undisturbed.
If you're a muscle cell in a chimpanzee (or any other animal) this has a lot of
advantages: you don't have a lot of energy as it is, so you're not expending
energy to feed yourself. The bloodstream has a higher concentration of the
nutrients that the cell needs than the cell itself, so passive diffusion moves
them outside the cell to inside the cell.

The downside about relying on passive diffusion is that there are limits on how
large you can be. The greater the surface area of a cell, the greater the rate
of materials that can diffuse in. But larger volumes require more time to
diffuse through. If you roughly approximate a cell as a sphere, surface area is
proportional to the radius squared while volume is proportional to the _cube_ of
radius. This means that cells are limited by how fast sustenance can
diffuse to where it needs to go within the cell: a smaller cell with a higher
surface-area-to-volume-ratio can get nutrients in with time to spare while
larger cells and a lower ratio will struggle.

I've always thought that this applies pretty well to companies. A ten person
company is a lot like a small cell with a high ratio. Stretching the analogy a
little, no part of the cell is that far from the outer cell membrane that
represents interactions with customers where you're trying to bring value.
Everyone at a sufficiently small company will understand the company's value
proposition, because they're not that far removed from the work that is done to
fulfil that value proposition: the owner of an auto repair shop can often
literally see the shop floor from their office, and at a small manufacturer it
doesn't take that much time for word of a serious problem to reach company
leadership whether it came from employees on the line or from a customer.

But the larger a company grows, the more of its parts are not directly tied to
the core work of the company: Google used to employ [ex-Pixar
screenwriters](https://www.wired.com/story/the-human-in-google-assistant/) to
make Google Assistant more engaging, and Tinder used to have an [in-house
sociologist](https://www.drjesscarbino.com/#about). Both of these jobs bring
value to the company, but the explanation as to _how_ they bring value is more
complicated than it would be be for any job at a 50 person company. But even for
less unusual jobs, the distance that most employees have from where the value is
being created and how long it takes feedback from the outside world to reach
them can drive diseconomies of scale. If an indie developer unknowingly
breaks a user's workflow they are likely to find out much faster than the
developers responsible for the depths of Microsoft Word: just validating a bug
and routing it to the right team isn't trivial at a large organisation and
unless something is seriously broken it can take a surprising amount of time.
